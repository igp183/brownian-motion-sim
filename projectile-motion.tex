\section{Physical Model}
\subsection{Motivation}
When we consider a projectile launched from ground level with an initial
velocity \(v_0\) at an angle \(\theta\) above the horizontal, the motion can be
described by Newton's second law. If we ask the question of which angle
provides the maximum range, in the absence of air resistance, the answer is
famously \(45^\circ\). However this is not the case if we include air
resistance.

\subsection{Newton's Second Law}
Newton's second law for the projectile reads
\begin{equation}
\vb{F}_r + \vb{F}_g = m\vb{a},
\end{equation}
where $\vb{F}_g = (0, -mg)$ is the force of gravity. Writing this
equation component by component,
\begin{equation}
    a_x = \frac{F_{r,x}}{m}, \qquad
    a_y = -g - \frac{F_{r,y}}{m},
\end{equation}
where $F_{r,x}$ and $F_{r,y}$ are still unknown, since we have not yet
written $\vb{F}_r$ explicitly.

\subsection{The Drag Force}
When air resistance is included, the projectile is subject to a drag force
$\vb{F}_r$ that always acts in the opposite direction of the velocity $\vb{v}$.
Throughout this document, bold symbols denote vectors (e.g. $\vb{v}$),
while the same symbol in italics denotes its magnitude (e.g.
$v = |\vb{v}|$).

The form of the drag force depends on the Reynolds number,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Re} = \frac{Dv\rho}{\eta},
\end{equation}
where $D$ is the projectile's size, $\rho$ the density of air, and
$\eta$ its viscosity.

$\mathrm{Re}$ is the ratio of inertial to viscous forces in the
surrounding flow. If we have a low $\mathrm{Re}$, viscous forces
dominate and the drag force is linear,
\begin{equation}
    F_r \propto v \qquad \text{(linear, or Stokes, drag)},
\end{equation}
which is a regime typical of small objects moving slowly 
through a viscous fluid, such as a droplet. This regime holds for
$\mathrm{Re} \lesssim 1$; for example, a $10\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ fog
droplet settling in air at roughly $2.7\ \mathrm{cm/s}$ has
$\mathrm{Re} \approx 0.016$ \cite{lubarda2022}. Turning the
proportionality into an equality introduces a constant $b$, the linear
drag coefficient, which for a sphere is given by Stokes' law as
$b = 3\pi\eta D$. Since the force points opposite to the velocity,
\begin{equation}
\vb{F}_r = -b\,\vb{v}.
\end{equation}

On the other hand, if we have a high $\mathrm{Re}$, inertial forces
dominate and the drag force is quadratic,
\begin{equation}
    F_r \propto v^2 \qquad \text{(quadratic, or Newtonian, drag)},
\end{equation}
which is a regime typical of bigger objects moving through air,
such as a thrown ball or a fired projectile. This regime holds for
speeds between approximately $0.25\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $53\ \mathrm{m/s}$,
corresponding to $10^3 < \mathrm{Re} < 2\times10^5$
\cite{chudinov2013}.

Between these two regimes, roughly $1 \lesssim \mathrm{Re} \lesssim 10^3$,
there is a middle zone where neither law works well, and the drag force
has no simple formula, instead being found from experiments or lookup
tables \cite{lubarda2022}. This middle zone does not matter here, since
projectile motion falls well inside the quadratic regime.

So far we only know that the drag force grows with the square of the
speed -- we do not yet have an exact formula. To turn this
proportionality into a real equation, we introduce a constant, $C$,
called the drag coefficient:
\begin{equation}
    F_r = C v^2.
\end{equation}
This constant packages together everything else that affects the
force: the projectile's shape, its cross-sectional area, and the
density of the air. In reality, $C$ can itself change slightly with
the speed of the projectile, $C = C(\vb{v})$. However, this variation
is small within the range of speeds we consider, so throughout this
document we treat $C$ as a fixed, known constant. This is an
approximation, but a reasonable one: it greatly simplifies the
equations while still capturing the essential physics of drag.

We still need the force as a vector, not just a size. To point it in
the right direction, we multiply by a unit vector -- a vector of
length one -- that points opposite to the motion. Dividing $\vb{v}$ by
its own length, $\vb{v}/v$, gives exactly that: it keeps the direction
of the velocity but has length one. So the drag force is
\begin{equation}
\vb{F}_r = -C\,v^2\,\frac{\vb{v}}{v} = -C\,v\,\vb{v},
\qquad v = |\vb{v}| = \sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2},
\end{equation}
where the minus sign flips the direction, and one factor of $v$
cancels against the denominator.

Substituting this into the component equations above,
\begin{equation}
    a_x = -\frac{C}{m}\,v\,v_x, \qquad
    a_y = -g - \frac{C}{m}\,v\,v_y.
    \label{eq:accel_components}
\end{equation}

\section{System of Differential Equations}

The equations for $a_x$ and $a_y$ derived above are completed by the
definitions of velocity and acceleration,
\begin{equation}
    \vb{v} = \frac{d\vb{r}}{dt}, \qquad
    \vb{a} = \frac{d\vb{v}}{dt} = \frac{d^2\vb{r}}{dt^2},
\end{equation}
together with the initial condition
\begin{equation}
    \vb{v}(t=0) = \vb{v}_0,
\end{equation}
where $\vb{v}_0$ is a given constant.

Written out, this gives the following system of differential
equations:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dv_x}{dt} = -\frac{C}{m}\,v\,v_x, \qquad
    \frac{dv_y}{dt} = -g - \frac{C}{m}\,v\,v_y,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dx}{dt} = v_x, \qquad
    \frac{dy}{dt} = v_y.
\end{equation}

In principle, this system cannot be solved analytically. This is due
to the nonlinear coupling introduced by $v = \sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}$:
unlike the linear drag case, where the equation for $v_x$ depends only
on $v_x$ and can be solved on its own, here the equation for $v_x$
also depends on $v_y$ (and vice versa), so the two cannot be solved
independently.

\section{Numerical Integration}

We have just shown that this system of differential equations cannot
be solved analytically. This does not mean the motion is unknown --
only that we cannot write $\vb{v}(t)$ and $\vb{r}(t)$ as a closed
formula. Instead, we approximate them numerically, by advancing the
motion one small step at a time rather than jumping straight to the
answer.

One way to do this is the following. We split the flight of the
projectile, of total duration $\tau$, into a large number of steps of
small duration $\Delta t$, say $\Delta t/\tau \leq 10^{-3}$, with
$\tau$ estimated from the case without air resistance. We then assume
that, within each such short step, $\vb{v}$ and $\vb{a}$ do not change
appreciably, i.e.\ they are approximately constant.

This assumption lets us reuse the constant-acceleration formulas from
introductory kinematics,
\begin{equation}
    v = v_0 + at, \qquad x = x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2,
    \label{eq:kinematics}
\end{equation}
but only over one short step at a time, since here $\vb{a}$ is not
truly constant -- it depends on the velocity, which keeps changing.
Concretely, this comes from the definition of acceleration,
$\vb{a} = d\vb{v}/dt$, which for a small but finite $\Delta t$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \vb{a} \approx \frac{\vb{v}(t+\Delta t) - \vb{v}(t)}{\Delta t}
    \quad \Longrightarrow \quad
    \vb{v}(t+\Delta t) \approx \vb{v}(t) + \vb{a}(t)\,\Delta t.
\end{equation}
Writing $\vb{v}_n$ for $\vb{v}(t_n)$, and $\vb{a}_n$ for the
acceleration evaluated using the velocity at step $n$ (from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:accel_components}), this is
\begin{equation}
    \vb{v}_{n+1} = \vb{v}_n + \vb{a}_n \Delta t, \qquad
    \vb{a}_n = \left(-\frac{C}{m}v_n v_{n,x},\; -g-\frac{C}{m}v_n
    v_{n,y}\right).
\end{equation}
In other words, we repeatedly apply the ordinary constant-acceleration
formula, but only for one short step, and we recompute $\vb{a}_n$ from
the updated velocity before taking the next step.

Similarly, for the position, the second formula in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:kinematics} gives
\begin{equation}
    \vb{r}_{n+1} = \vb{r}_n + \vb{v}_n \Delta t +
    \frac{1}{2}\vb{a}_n(\Delta t)^2.
\end{equation}
The last term is negligible compared to the first as $\Delta t \to
0$. To see why, compare the two terms for a typical speed
$v \sim 20\ \mathrm{m/s}$, acceleration $a \sim 10\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$,
and $\Delta t = 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s}$:
\begin{equation}
    v\,\Delta t \sim 2\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m},
    \qquad
    \tfrac{1}{2}a(\Delta t)^2 \sim 5\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m}.
\end{equation}
The quadratic term is smaller than the linear one by a factor on the
order of $\Delta t$ itself, since it carries an extra power of
$\Delta t$; for $\Delta t \ll 1$, squaring makes it far smaller still.
This is why the term is said to be of order $O[(\Delta t)^2]$: it
shrinks much faster than the linear term as $\Delta t$ is made
smaller, and can safely be dropped, leaving
\begin{equation}
    \vb{r}_{n+1} = \vb{r}_n + \vb{v}_n \Delta t.
\end{equation}

\section{Stopping Condition}

The scheme above advances the projectile step by step for as long as we
let it. What it does not tell us is \emph{when to stop}. The projectile
has landed when its height returns to zero, and the horizontal distance
covered at that moment is the range, $d$. We therefore need a test,
applied at every step, that tells us whether the ground has been
reached.

\subsection{A test based on the vertical velocity}

One natural test comes from the case without air resistance. There the
vertical velocity is simply
\begin{equation}
    v_y(t) = v_{0,y} - g t,
\end{equation}
which starts positive as the projectile climbs, passes through zero at
the top, and turns negative on the way down. The trajectory is a
parabola, symmetric about its peak, so the projectile comes back down
exactly as fast as it went up: at the moment it lands, $v_y$ has
reached $-v_{0,y}$. This gives a possible stopping test,
\begin{equation}
    (v_y)_{n+1} \leq -v_{0,y},
    \label{eq:stop_vy}
\end{equation}
which is convenient, since $v_y$ is already computed at every step.

\subsection{What drag does to this test}

Drag opposes the motion at all times, so it brakes the projectile on
the way up \emph{and} on the way down. The up-down symmetry of the
parabola is therefore lost. Because drag continuously removes energy
from the motion and never returns any, the projectile arrives at the
ground moving more slowly than it left it,
\begin{equation}
    |v_{y,\text{landing}}| < v_{0,y}
    \qquad \text{whenever } C > 0 ,
    \label{eq:landing_bound}
\end{equation}
with equality only in the drag-free case.

This means Eq.~\eqref{eq:stop_vy} is not met at the instant of
landing. It may still be met slightly \emph{after} it: below the
ground the projectile keeps speeding up, and $|v_y|$ may yet reach
$v_{0,y}$. Whether it does depends on the terminal velocity
$v_t = \sqrt{mg/C}$, the largest downward speed drag permits:
\begin{itemize}
    \item if $v_t > v_{0,y}$ (weak drag), the test is met a little
    below ground, and the simulation stops slightly late;
    \item if $v_t \leq v_{0,y}$ (strong drag), it is never met, and
    the simulation does not stop on its own.
\end{itemize}
In the first case the overshoot is small, and this is also the regime
where the $45^\circ$ result is recovered. The overshoot grows with
$C$, however, and every step taken below ground still adds to $x$, so
the range comes out slightly too large.

\subsection{A test based on the height}

The most direct test is to ask the question itself: the projectile has
landed when its height is no longer positive,
\begin{equation}
    y_{n+1} \leq 0 .
    \label{eq:stop_y}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Comparing the two}

Rather than choose between them, we implement both and let the
simulation measure the difference. The loop stops when
\begin{equation}
    \underbrace{(v_y)_{n+1} \leq -v_{0,y}}_{\text{velocity test}}
    \quad\text{or}\quad
    \underbrace{y_{n+1} \leq 0}_{\text{height test}}
    \quad\text{or}\quad
    \underbrace{n > n_{\max}}_{\text{step limit}} ,
\end{equation}
recording which one triggered. The step limit is required because, as
shown above, the velocity test alone may never be met.

Running both across a range of $C$ then measures the discrepancy
rather than assuming it. We expect the relative difference in range,
$(d_{v_y} - d_y)/d_y$, to vanish as $C \to 0$ and to grow with $C$;
where it becomes appreciable is precisely where the two tests stop
agreeing. It is also worth checking whether the optimal angle
$\theta$ is affected at all, since a bias in $d$ does not necessarily
shift the angle that maximises it.

Once the loop stops, the range is $d = x_{n+1}$. Since the final step
generally carries the projectile just past $y = 0$, a more accurate
value is obtained by interpolating linearly between the last point
above the ground and the first point below it.

\section{How this turns into Code}

This section outlines how the equations above map onto a program. It
is intentionally abstracted: the purpose is to define the structure 
and the reasoning behind it, not the syntax. For the simulation itself,
the language chosen was C++, but the same structure could be implemented 
in any language.

\subsection{Reducing the parameters}

Before writing anything, it is worth noticing that $C$ and $m$ never
appear separately in the equations of motion, only ever in the
combination $C/m$. Defining
\begin{equation}
    k \equiv \frac{C}{m},
\end{equation}
the mass drops out of the dynamics, and the accelerations
become
\begin{equation}
    a_x = -k\,v\,v_x, \qquad a_y = -g - k\,v\,v_y .
    \label{eq:accel_k}
\end{equation}
The terminal velocity also simplifies to $v_t = \sqrt{g/k}$.

The value of $k$ is not arbitrary. Comparing $F_r = Cv^2$ with the
standard drag equation $F_r = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_d A v^2$ gives
\begin{equation}
    C = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,C_d\,A ,
    \qquad\text{so}\qquad
    k = \frac{\rho\,C_d\,A}{2m},
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the density of air, $C_d$ a dimensionless
shape-dependent coefficient, and $A$ the frontal area. A program can
therefore expose $k$ at three levels: as a named preset, as a single
number, or as the individual quantities $\rho$, $C_d$, $A$ and $m$
from which it is computed.

For interpreting results, however, neither $C$ nor $k$ is the natural
parameter, since neither tells us how strong the drag is compared to
gravity. That comparison is made by the dimensionless group
\begin{equation}
    \beta \equiv \frac{k\,v_0^2}{g} = \left(\frac{v_0}{v_t}\right)^2 ,
\end{equation}
To see that $\beta$ is dimensionless, recall that $k$ has units of
inverse length, $[k] = \mathrm{m}^{-1}$, since it multiplies $v\,v_x$
(units $\mathrm{m}^2/\mathrm{s}^2$) to produce an acceleration (units
$\mathrm{m}/\mathrm{s}^2$). Then
\begin{equation}
    [\beta] = [k]\,\frac{[v_0]^2}{[g]}
    = \mathrm{m}^{-1} \cdot
    \frac{\mathrm{m}^2/\mathrm{s}^2}{\mathrm{m}/\mathrm{s}^2}
    = \mathrm{m}^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{m} = 1,
\end{equation}
confirming that $\beta$ is a pure number.

\subsection{Structure}

The natural decomposition follows the derivation itself. Each layer
uses only the one below it:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acceleration}: given a state $(x, y, v_x, v_y)$
    and $k$, return $(a_x, a_y)$. This is based on Eq.~\eqref{eq:accel_k}.
    \item \textbf{Step}: given a state, $\Delta t$ and $k$, return
    the state one step later.
    \item \textbf{Trajectory}: given launch conditions, step until
    a stopping test fires; return the range and which test fired.
    \item \textbf{Sweeps}: run trajectories over a grid of
    $\theta$ to find the optimal angle, and over a grid of $\beta$ to
    see how that angle varies.
\end{itemize}
Each of these is a pure function: its output depends only on its
inputs. This keeps them independently testable
and makes the parallelism in the next section trivially safe.

Two structural points are worth fixing early. First, the physics core
should not depend on any graphics library; rendering may depend on
the core, never the reverse. This allows the same code to run
headless for large sweeps and interactively for exploration. Second,
because the innermost functions are called millions of times, they
should be defined in headers so the compiler can inline them; the
layering above is for human readers, and should cost nothing at
runtime.

\subsection{Choosing $\Delta t$}

The criterion $\Delta t/\tau \leq 10^{-3}$ requires an estimate of the
flight time $\tau$, taken from the drag-free case,
\begin{equation}
    \tau \approx \frac{2 v_0 \sin\theta}{g} .
\end{equation}
Since $\tau$ depends on $\theta$, so does the appropriate $\Delta t$.
Deriving it per trajectory gives every angle a comparable number of
steps and a comparable relative error, whereas a single global
$\Delta t$ leaves shallow angles over-resolved and steep ones
under-resolved. When $\Delta t$ is instead supplied directly, the
program should check the criterion and warn when it is violated,
which will happen for near-horizontal launches, where $\tau \to 0$.

\subsection{Performance}

Two decisions dominate, and both are structural.

The first is to avoid storing trajectories during sweeps. Only the
final range is needed, so the entire state is four numbers and no
memory needs to be allocated at all. Only the interactive mode
requires the full path, and it draws only a handful of trajectories.

The second is where to parallelise. The Euler loop is inherently
sequential, each step depends on the previous one, and must not
be split. The sweeps, by contrast, consist of completely independent
trajectories, one per $(\beta, \theta)$ pair. Flattening that grid
into a single loop and distributing it across threads is safe
precisely because the trajectory function is pure.

\subsection{Comparing the stopping tests}

The stopping test does not affect the dynamics; it only decides when
we stop reading the result. A single trajectory can therefore record
the step at which \emph{each} test would have fired, and report both
ranges. This halves the work relative to simulating twice, and
guarantees the two are compared on the same trajectory rather than on
two separately computed ones.

Whether the run ends at the first or the last test to fire can then be
a user-facing option, letting the same simulation serve both as the
result and as the measurement of the discrepancy discussed in
Section~4.

\subsection{Validation}

Two independent checks are available, and both should be run before
any result is trusted.

The first is physical: as $\beta \to 0$, the optimal angle must
approach $45^\circ$. This tests whether the model has been
implemented correctly.

The second is numerical: running the same case with $\Delta t$,
$\Delta t/2$ and $\Delta t/4$ should show the answer settling down.
Since Euler's method is first order, the change between successive
refinements should itself halve each time. If it does not, the
integrator is wrong, regardless of how plausible the trajectories
look. This plays the same role that a known analytical limit plays
for the physics.

\subsection{Visualisation}

The results that matter here are curves rather than motion: the range
as a function of launch angle, $d(\theta)$, and the optimal angle as
a function of drag, $\theta_{\mathrm{opt}}(\beta)$. For a written
report these are best produced from exported data.

There is, however, one genuinely interactive element. Although a
single trajectory is static once computed, the \emph{family} of
trajectories is not: varying $\beta$ continuously deforms the whole
fan of paths and slides the optimal angle away from $45^\circ$. An
interactive view showing a fan of trajectories at different launch
angles, with the optimal one highlighted, alongside a live plot of
$d(\theta)$, makes the central result of this project directly
visible. Recomputing a few dozen trajectories per frame is negligible
work, so this costs nothing in performance.

Both modes should share the same core, differing only in what they do
with the results: write them to a file, or draw them.
